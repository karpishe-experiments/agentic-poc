# Guideline: Generating a Children's Book Story Outline

**Artifact:** 02 — Story Outline  
**Status:** Draft guideline for review  
**Purpose:** Turn an approved Book Concept Brief into a coherent, age-appropriate sequence of narrative beats. This is a planning artifact, not the child-facing manuscript or page layout.

## Inputs required

- The **approved Book Concept Brief**
- The intended **reader age or age range**
- Any user-approved creative decisions made after the brief

If the concept brief is not approved, do not produce a final outline. First present it as a draft direction and obtain approval.

## Output template

```md
# Story Outline — [Working Title]

**Artifact:** 02 — Story Outline  
**Status:** Draft awaiting approval | Approved  
**Audience:** Ages [X–Y / X]  
**Based on:** [Approved Concept Brief filename or title]

## Narrative premise

[One or two sentences restating the approved story engine.]

## Emotional arc

- **Opening state:** [How the protagonist feels / what is missing]
- **Pressure or disruption:** [What changes]
- **Choice:** [The meaningful action the protagonist chooses]
- **Ending state:** [How the protagonist or their world has changed]

## Story beats

1. **Opening / normal world** — [establish protagonist, setting, need]
2. **Inciting incident** — [a specific event starts the story]
3. **First response** — [protagonist reacts, explores, or attempts]
4. **Escalation** — [a clear obstacle or consequence raises stakes]
5. **Climax / decisive choice** — [protagonist actively resolves the core conflict]
6. **Resolution** — [show consequences and emotional payoff]

## Continuity rules

- [Rules the story must preserve from the concept brief]

## Intentionally unresolved

- [Details deferred to character, world, manuscript, or layout artifacts]

## Review questions

1. [Only questions that need a user decision before approval.]
```

## Method

1. **Extract the locked requirements.** Identify the audience age, core premise, emotional promise, required representation, tone, and boundaries from the approved concept brief. Do not silently replace them.
2. **Define one causal chain.** Every major beat must lead naturally to the next: normal world → disruption → response → complication → choice → result.
3. **Give the protagonist agency.** The protagonist must make the key choice at the climax. Avoid rescues that happen because an adult, coincidence, or a new power solves everything for them.
4. **Match the age group.** Use a comprehensible goal, contained stakes, limited subplots, and an emotionally reassuring resolution appropriate to the stated age.
5. **Keep the outline at planning altitude.** Describe actions, turning points, and emotional movement. Do not write final narration, dialogue, page breaks, illustration instructions, costume specifications, or detailed world lore unless already locked.
6. **Preserve respectful representation.** When disability, culture, family structure, identity, or other lived experience is part of the premise, make it a natural dimension of the character. Do not frame identity as a defect to repair, a magical explanation, or the sole source of worth unless the user explicitly asks for that theme.
7. **Expose open decisions.** List unresolved details instead of inventing them. End with a short set of high-value review questions only when they are needed to approve the outline.
8. **Set the correct status.** Label the first version `Draft awaiting approval`. Change it to `Approved` only after the user explicitly approves it.

## Quality checks

- Audience age is explicitly stated and reflected in stakes, language, and complexity.
- The outline directly follows the approved concept brief.
- The inciting incident is clear and occurs early.
- The conflict escalates through cause and effect.
- The climax comes from the protagonist's meaningful action.
- The ending resolves both the external problem and the emotional arc.
- No detailed manuscript, pagination, or illustration plan has been prematurely added.
- Status is correct: draft until explicit user approval.

## Common failures to avoid

- Introducing a different premise, power, villain, lesson, or tone than the approved brief.
- Treating a character trait or disability as a problem to be cured or a shortcut to specialness.
- Writing a sequence of cool events with no causal connection.
- Giving the protagonist no real choice in the climax.
- Adding many side characters, villains, locations, or subplots before the story engine is stable.
- Presenting the outline as approved without an explicit user decision.
